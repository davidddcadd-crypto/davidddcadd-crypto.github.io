  1. 01 — Roles

    Whose side is each agent on?

    Today a brand reaches you through ads, search and its own website, and you compare and check out alone. The proposal gives each side an agent of its own: a Personal AI that works for you, and a Brand AI that works for the merchant.

  2. 02 — Privacy

    Share a brief, not a history

    In the proposal, your full conversation with your Personal AI would stay on your side. Only a short brief you have agreed to share — intent, requirements, budget, timing, location scope and an expiry — would cross to the seller.

  3. 03 — Negotiation

    The agents negotiate, and you can follow it

    An illustrative example, not a recording. Your Personal AI verifies stock, rejects an auto-renewal and asks for a better offer; the Brand AI won't cut the sticker price but offers a 3-year warranty. Your agent says upfront cost matters more and asks for a waived setup fee or store credit — and secures CAD 50 credit with setup waived.

  4. 04 — Sponsorship

    Access may be sponsored; judgment should not be

    A brand could pay for discovery, for a relevant moment or for a richer conversation with its Brand AI, with its offer clearly marked as sponsored. It could not buy a place in your Personal AI's ranking or reach into your private memory.

  5. 05 — Approval

    You approve; your agent keeps watching

    Your Personal AI would bring back a plain summary — no price cut, but CAD 50 credit and the setup fee waived — and ask whether to accept. Nothing would be bought until you say yes; after that, it would follow the transaction through delivery, refunds and warranty.

02 Concept

Problem

If AI agents begin to shop and sell on people's behalf, the first question is whose side each agent is on. If the assistant a person relies on can quietly be paid to favour a product, it is no longer really their assistant — and once people suspect that, they have little reason to keep trusting it.

Selling is not generic. Every brand has its own pricing rules, negotiation authority, discounts, financing, bundles, trade-ins, loyalty treatment, and return and warranty policy. That logic belongs to the seller, stated openly, not folded invisibly into the buyer's assistant.

The buyer needs an agent of their own: one that understands what they actually need, compares offers, challenges claims, rejects bad terms and stays involved after the sale. And it should be able to do that without handing the merchant the buyer's whole private conversation.

Architecture

A proposed two-sided architecture. A Personal AI acts for the buyer and a Brand AI acts for the seller. They meet in a commerce channel where offers, policy, consent and verification pass between them. Only a minimal, permissioned brief crosses from the buyer's side, and the buyer approves any purchase.

  1. 01Personal AI (buyer side)Understands the user's requirements, filters brands, compares offers, challenges claims, rejects bad terms and negotiates where permitted.
  2. 02Commercial briefA minimal, permissioned handoff: intent, requirements, budget, timing, location scope, any context the user explicitly approves, and an expiry. Not the user's conversation history.
  3. 03Commerce channelWhere the two agents exchange offers, policy, counter-offers and verification. Sponsored offers may enter here, always marked as sponsored.
  4. 04Brand AI (seller side)Carries one brand's selling logic openly: pricing rules, negotiation authority, discounts, financing, bundles, trade-ins, loyalty treatment, and return and warranty policy.
  5. 05Human approvalThe Personal AI summarizes the outcome in plain language; the user decides whether to accept. Nothing would be bought without that approval.
  6. 06Post-purchase supervisionAfter approval, the Personal AI oversees payment and checkout, then follows delivery, refunds and warranty obligations.

Design principles

  • Each agent has one principal

    The Personal AI works for the buyer. The Brand AI works for the seller and is identified as such. Neither presents itself as the other.

  • Selling logic belongs to the brand

    Pricing, discounts, financing and negotiation authority differ from brand to brand, so they live in an explicitly identified Brand AI, not inside the buyer's assistant.

  • Share a brief, not a history

    Only what the user has agreed to share crosses to the seller, with a defined scope and an expiry. Unrelated private memory stays on the user's side.

  • Access may be sponsored; judgment should not be

    Sponsorship may pay for discovery, for access to a relevant commercial moment or for a richer brand interaction. It should not buy the Personal AI's organic judgment, hidden ranking or access to unrelated private memory.

  • Trust comes from visible advocacy

    The user should be able to see their agent working for them — what it checked, rejected and secured — rather than being asked to trust it on feeling alone.

  • The human approves; the agent keeps watching

    The user makes the decision to buy. The Personal AI then supervises what follows: payment, delivery, refunds and warranty.

Limitations & current status

  • This is a concept and architecture proposal, not a deployed product. Nothing on this page describes a system in use.
  • The central idea — that buyer-side and seller-side agents should be kept separate — is a hypothesis, not a finding.
  • The negotiation shown above is an illustrative example written to explain the idea. It is not a recording of real agents, merchants or prices.
  • This page describes the idea and its design; it does not report measured results.
  • Open question, not a settled standard: should buyer agents and seller agents have explicit role boundaries?
  • Open question: what information should cross between them?
  • Open question: how should negotiation authority be represented?
  • Open question: can sponsorship buy access without buying judgment?
  • Open question: what memory and evidence layer does a personal AI need to represent a person safely over time?
