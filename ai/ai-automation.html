  1. 01 — Intake

    Real work arrives messy

    Work rarely arrives as a clean brief. In this illustrative, anonymized example, a small shop sends a dim, off-centre product photo and a one-line note — “New stock in. Can we post it?” — with no caption, no plan and no channel.

  2. 02 — Analyze & plan

    Analyze, then plan

    First the workflow works out what it has: a handmade mug on a shop counter, in light too dim to use as it is. Then it plans the output — a new-arrival post for the shop's social feed, timed for the next weekday morning.

  3. 03 — Generate

    Draft as structured output

    The draft is a structured result, not loose text: an enhanced image (brightened and cropped), a caption — “New in: hand-glazed mugs. Best in the city.” — a channel, a time, and a status that still reads “draft, not sent”. Because each part is a separate field, each part can be checked on its own.

  4. 04 — Review

    Review gates catch problems

    Checks run before anyone is asked to decide. The structure is valid and the image passes the aesthetic review, but the claim review flags “best in the city” as unsupported — so the draft is held for the owner with the flag attached, and nothing is sent.

  5. 05 — Owner approval

    The owner decides

    The owner can approve, modify or reject; reject stops the work and nothing is sent. Here the owner chooses modify and asks for the claim to be removed, so the draft loops back, comes out as version 2 (“New in: hand-glazed mugs.”), passes the checks again, and only then is approved.

  6. 06 — Execute

    Only then, act

    The step past the owner's gate is connected only after approval. The approved version 2 is posted to the social feed at the planned time — and only that version goes out.

  7. 07 — Record

    Everything is recorded and followed up

    Each step leaves an entry: input received, analyzed, plan set, draft created, claim flagged, held for the owner, the owner's modify and approve decisions, and the post. A follow-up task — check the replies — means the work does not end at “sent”, and who approved what stays on file.

02 Concept

Problem

Real-world work rarely arrives clean: it starts as a photo, a request or a message, and has to be understood, planned, produced and checked before anything happens. Automating that chain is useful only if the owner stays in control of what actually goes out.

This work builds workflows that carry a task from intake to action — with review gates, owner approval and exception handling designed in, rather than bolted on afterwards.

Architecture

A staged workflow. Each stage hands a reviewable, structured result to the next; review gates check it; the owner approves, modifies or rejects; and only an approved result is executed and recorded. The workflows are built with tools such as n8n, local models and AI-assisted tooling; the example above is a generic illustration of the pattern, not a specific client system.

  1. 01IntakeReceives the input: an image or asset, a request or a message.
  2. 02AnalysisWorks out what the input is and what it needs.
  3. 03PlanningDecides the steps and the output required.
  4. 04Generate / prepareProduces content or visuals, or prepares an operational action, as structured output.
  5. 05Review gatesChecks such as structured-output validation, claim review and aesthetic review; validation failures and exceptions are surfaced, not hidden.
  6. 06Owner approvalAPPROVE, MODIFY or REJECT. Modify sends the work back with a correction; reject stops it. Nothing is published or executed before this step.
  7. 07ExecuteThe approved result is published or the approved action is taken.
  8. 08Record & follow upWhat happened, and who approved it, is recorded so it can be followed up.

Design principles

  • The owner decides

    Approve, modify and reject are first-class controls, not afterthoughts. The workflow is designed so that nothing is published or executed until the owner approves.

  • Structured, so it can be checked

    Drafts are produced as structured output, so each part can be validated and reviewed on its own before a person is asked to decide.

  • Designed for real operations

    Workflows are designed around the operational steps a business actually runs, including the awkward ones.

  • Exceptions are part of the design

    Validation and exception handling sit alongside human review, not after it.

  • Anonymized by default

    Examples omit client identities and commercial details unless the client has approved them.

Limitations & current status

  • The workflow shown above is an illustrative, anonymized example written to explain the pattern. It does not show a client system, client data or real results.
  • Client identities, contact details, messages, platform accounts and commercial terms are not shown.
  • This page describes the workflow pattern and its controls; it does not report measured results.
  • Review gates and owner approval are design controls that reduce the chance of an unwanted post or action; they are not a guarantee against errors.
